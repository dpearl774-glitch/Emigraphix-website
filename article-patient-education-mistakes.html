5 Mistakes That Make Patient Education Materials Hard to Understand

Small design and writing choices can make useful information feel harder than it needs to be.

Patient education materials often start with good intentions: give people everything they need to know. The problem is that “everything” can quickly become a wall of information.

If the material feels tiring before someone even starts reading, useful information can be missed. These are five common problems worth checking before a handout, poster or infographic goes out.

1. Trying to explain too much at once

Not every fact needs to live on the same page. Decide what the person needs to know now and what can be moved to another page, conversation or resource.

CDC recommends breaking information into logical chunks and using headings. Smaller, related sections feel easier to approach than one long block of text.

2. Using professional language with a public audience

Words that are normal in a clinic may be unfamiliar outside it. If a simpler everyday word keeps the meaning accurate, use it. Where a technical term is necessary, explain it.

This is not “dumbing down” information. It is making sure the message reaches the person it was written for.

3. Making everything visually important

Too many colours, bold headings, boxes, icons and type sizes can make a page harder to follow. Good hierarchy gives the reader somewhere to start and a clear path through the information.

Choose what deserves emphasis. Let supporting information look like supporting information.

4. Using visuals that do not add meaning

Pictures can help people understand and remember information, but decorative images can also take up space without helping. CDC recommends visuals that support the main message and have clear labels or captions where needed.

If removing an image changes nothing about how well the material communicates, ask whether it needs to be there.

5. Forgetting the next step

After reading the material, what should the person do? A patient handout may need a reminder to speak with their healthcare professional. A screening poster may need a booking number. A campaign graphic may need a registration link.

Make that action easy to find. A useful QR code can help when it removes a step, but it should not be the only way to access essential information.

Before publishing: read the material as if you are seeing the topic for the first time. Can you identify the main message quickly? Are unfamiliar terms explained? Is the next action obvious?

Patient education design is not about fitting more onto a page. It is about helping the right information stand out.
