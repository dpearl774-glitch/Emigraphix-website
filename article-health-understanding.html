Why Health Information Should Be Designed for Understanding, Not Just Beauty

Good health design is not decoration. It helps people find the message, understand it and act on it.

It is easy to judge a health flyer, poster or patient handout by one question: Does it look good? That matters, but it is not enough.

Health communication has another job. The person looking at the material should be able to find the message, understand what it means and know what to do with it.

Good-looking information can still be difficult information

A design may have polished colours, beautiful illustrations and a modern layout while still using unexplained medical terms, tiny type or six messages fighting for attention. In that case, the design is attractive but the communication is weak.

WHO describes effective health communication as understandable, relevant, actionable, accessible, timely and credible. Its guidance on understandable communication specifically recommends clear language, familiar words and visuals that reinforce the main message.

Start with the audience, not the layout

Before designing, ask who the information is for. A poster for health professionals can use language that would be unsuitable for a community audience. A patient education handout for newly diagnosed patients may need more explanation than a reminder poster in a clinic waiting area.

The audience affects the wording, the amount of information, the type of visuals and even what should be emphasised first.

Make the main message easy to find

CDC's plain-language guidance recommends putting the most important information first and breaking content into logical chunks with headings. That is as much a design decision as a writing decision.

Clear headings, enough spacing and a sensible reading order help the eye understand how the information fits together. The reader should not have to guess where to begin.

Visuals should explain, not decorate

Illustrations, diagrams and icons are useful when they make an idea easier to understand or remember. CDC notes that visuals can reinforce health messages, but they need clear labels and should support the main message.

That is why adding a random stethoscope, heart icon or stock photograph does not automatically improve health communication. The visual should earn its place.

The action matters too

Sometimes a health material explains a problem clearly but never tells the audience what happens next. Should they book an appointment? Speak to a health professional? Attend a screening? Call a number? Change a simple behaviour?

If there is an action, make it visible.

Our approach at Emigraphix: we do not treat health communication as ordinary promotional design with a medical icon added. We look at the message, audience, hierarchy and intended action before deciding how the design should look.

Beauty and clarity are not opposites. The best health materials can have both. The difference is that the visual choices are serving the message rather than competing with it.
